Pharm D

What pharmacy is and where it came from

Pharmacy is the health profession concerned with the preparation, supply and safe use of medicines. A Pharm D student starts by learning how the profession was formed, because the rules that govern dispensing today are the result of a long history of trial, error and regulation.

Key ideas

  • Pharmacy began with the collection and preparation of natural materials by apothecaries.
  • The word pharmacy comes from the Greek pharmakon, meaning a drug or poison.
  • Early practice was a craft: powders, ointments and infusions were made by hand.
  • The separation of pharmacy from medicine gave the pharmacist a distinct role.
  • Modern pharmacy is regulated by law, professional bodies and codes of ethics.
  • Ghanaian pharmacy practice is shaped by the Pharmacy and Poisons Board and the Faculty of Pharmacy and Pharmaceutical Sciences at KNUST.
  • The pharmacists role now includes dispensing, counselling, medicines information, clinical support and public health.

How it works

The historical progression explains current practice. Once medicines were prepared in a shop, the pharmacist had to control quality. Once medicines became industrial products, the pharmacist became the person who checks, selects and explains them. That is why the orientation lectures put so much weight on professional responsibility and why dispensing is taught as a clinical act rather than a counting exercise.

A useful mental picture is the medicines chain: discovery, manufacture, importation, storage, dispensing, use and disposal. The pharmacist has a duty at every link, and the law assigns specific duties at several of them.

  2. Name three modern roles of a pharmacist beyond dispensing.
  3. Why is professional ethics part of a first-year pharmacy course?

Answers: 1. To give the preparation and supply of medicines a dedicated, accountable profession. 2. Medicines information, counselling, clinical support. 3. Because pharmacists handle substances that can harm as well as heal.

Introduction to dosage forms

A dosage form is the physical shape in which a medicine is presented to the patient. The active ingredient is rarely given alone; the formulation determines how much reaches the site of action and how quickly.

Key ideas

  • A drug is the active substance; a dosage form is the vehicle that delivers it.
  • Excipients are inactive ingredients added for bulk, stability, taste, appearance or release control.
  • Solid forms include tablets, capsules and powders.
  • Liquid forms include solutions, suspensions and emulsions.
  • Semisolid forms include ointments, creams and gels.
  • Parenteral forms are injected.
  • The route and the dosage form are chosen together.
  • A well-designed form protects the drug, masks taste, measures the dose and improves adherence.

How it works

Start with the clinical need. A rapid effect may call for an injection or a solution; a prolonged effect may call for a tablet or a depot injection. Then consider the drug: is it soluble, stable in water, irritant to the stomach, or destroyed by acid? The formulation must answer these problems.

Excipients are not harmless by default. A colourant may cause a reaction, a preservative may irritate, and a filler may affect dissolution. This is why the pharmacist reads the full formulation, not just the strength on the label.

Check yourself

  1. What is the difference between a drug and a dosage form?
  2. Give two functions of an excipient.
  3. Why might a drug be formulated as a suspension rather than a solution?

Answers: 1. The drug is active; the form delivers it. 2. Bulk and stability. 3. Because the drug is poorly soluble or unstable in solution.

Galenical and non-galenical aqueous dosage forms

Galenical preparations are classical extracts of plant or animal material. They remain relevant in Ghana, where herbal and conventional practice overlap.

Key ideas

  • Galenical dosage forms include infusions, decoctions, tinctures, extracts and syrups.
  • An infusion is made by steeping plant material in hot water; a decoction is boiled.
  • A tincture uses alcohol as the solvent and is more concentrated than an infusion.
  • Non-galenical aqueous forms include solutions, syrups, elixirs and aromatic waters.
  • A syrup is a concentrated sugar solution that masks bitter taste.
  • An elixir is a sweetened hydroalcoholic liquid.
  • Aromatic waters are saturated solutions of volatile oils.
  • Extraction depends on the solvent, particle size, time and temperature.

How it works

Choose the extraction method to match the plant part. Soft leaves give infusions; barks and roots need decoction. Alcohol extracts resins and alkaloids that water will not take up. Too much heat destroys volatile oils, so aromatic material is added late or distilled.

Syrups resist microbial growth because of their high sugar concentration, but a dilute syrup must be preserved. Store liquids in well-closed containers away from light when the drug is light-sensitive.

Check yourself

  1. How does a tincture differ from an infusion?
  2. Why is sugar useful in a syrup besides taste?
  3. Name two factors that affect extraction from a plant.

Answers: 1. Tincture uses alcohol and is more concentrated. 2. It raises osmotic pressure and discourages microbial growth. 3. Solvent and particle size.

Parenterals

Parenteral dosage forms are given by injection, bypassing the gastrointestinal tract and the first-pass effect. That advantage comes with a strict requirement: the product must be sterile.

Key ideas

  • Routes include intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous and intradermal.
  • Intravenous injections must be sterile, pyrogen-free and free of visible particles.
  • Pyrogens are fever-causing substances, usually bacterial endotoxins.
  • Isotonicity reduces pain and tissue damage.
  • pH should be close to physiological where possible.
  • Preservatives are needed in multi-dose vials but not in single-dose ampoules.
  • Sterilisation methods include autoclaving, dry heat, filtration and radiation.
  • Aseptic technique is required while the product is exposed.

How it works

Prepare the formulation, then sterilise it by a method the drug can tolerate. Heat-labile drugs are filtered through a bacteria-retaining filter instead of autoclaved. Check the container for leaks and the solution for particles before release.

For the patient, the pharmacist must select the correct route, volume, needle and site. An injection given into the wrong layer may be painful or ineffective, so the anatomy of the injection site matters as much as the formula.

Check yourself

  1. Why must intravenous preparations be pyrogen-free?
  2. When would filtration be chosen over autoclaving?
  3. Why do multi-dose vials need preservatives?

Answers: 1. Pyrogens cause fever and shock when injected directly into the blood. 2. When the drug is destroyed by heat. 3. Because the container is opened repeatedly and may become contaminated.

The prescription and dispensing

A prescription is a legal order from a prescriber to a pharmacist. Dispensing is the process of interpreting that order, preparing the medicine and advising the patient.

Key ideas

  • A prescription normally carries the patients name and address, the date, the prescribers details, the medicine, the strength, the dose, the route, the frequency and the duration.
  • The superscription, inscription, subscription and signa are the traditional parts of a prescription.
  • Prescriptions are either extemporaneous or prepared products.
  • The pharmacist checks identity, dose, interactions, allergies and legality.
  • Repetition and refills are controlled by law and professional judgement.
  • A controlled drug requires stricter records.
  • Patient counselling is part of dispensing, not an optional extra.

How it works

Read the whole prescription before writing anything. Confirm the patient, then the drug and strength, then the directions. Calculate the quantity needed and check the dose against the accepted range for the patients age and weight. If something is unclear, contact the prescriber rather than guessing.

When labelling, write directions in plain language the patient can follow. State what the medicine is for, how much to take, when to take it, what to avoid and what to do if a problem occurs.

Check yourself

  1. What is the signa of a prescription?
  2. What should a pharmacist do when a dose looks unsafe?
  3. Give two items of advice to include on a label.

Answers: 1. The directions to the patient. 2. Contact the prescriber and do not dispense until resolved. 3. Dose and timing; common side effects.

Posology and dose calculation

Posology is the science of doses. It asks how much of a medicine to give, how often, and for how long.

Key ideas

  • A dose is the amount given at one time; the dosage schedule includes frequency and duration.
  • Dose is influenced by age, body weight, surface area, route, tolerance, genetics, elimination and interactions.
  • paediatric doses are often calculated from weight or body surface area.
  • Elderly patients may need reduction because of reduced clearance.
  • Renal or hepatic impairment changes elimination and therefore dose.
  • The therapeutic window lies between the minimum effective concentration and the minimum toxic concentration.
  • Loading doses fill the compartment quickly; maintenance doses replace what is eliminated.

How it works

Identify the calculation method the question expects. Weight-based dose is dose per kilogram multiplied by weight. Body surface area uses a nomogram or a formula, then dose per square metre. Strength and volume questions use ratio and proportion.

Always state units at every step. A mistake in converting milligrams to micrograms is a common and dangerous error. After calculating, check that the result is plausible for the drug and the patient.

Worked example

A child weighs 18 kg and the dose is 10 mg/kg per day in three divided doses. Daily dose = 180 mg, so each dose is 60 mg. If the suspension is 120 mg/5 mL, each dose is 60/120 x 5 = 2.5 mL. Check: 2.5 mL three times a day delivers 180 mg per day.

Check yourself

  1. Why do elderly patients often need lower doses?
  2. What is the therapeutic window?
  3. A dose is 5 mg/kg for a 24 kg child. Find the dose.

Answers: 1. Reduced renal and hepatic clearance and changed body composition. 2. The concentration range between effective and toxic. 3. 120 mg.

Pharmaceutical calculations

Most dispensing calculations are applications of ratio, proportion and unit conversion. Accuracy matters because the final product goes into a patient.

Key ideas

  • Concentration may be expressed as percentage, ratio strength, molarity or parts per million.
  • A percentage w/v means grams per 100 mL; w/w means grams per 100 g; v/v means mL per 100 mL.
  • Dilution uses C1 V1 = C2 V2.
  • An aliquot is a measured portion, used when the amount to be weighed is too small.
  • Displacement value accounts for the volume occupied by a powder in a suppository or capsule.
  • Alligation is used to mix two strengths to obtain a third.
  • Isotonicity calculations use freezing point depression or sodium chloride equivalents.

How it works

Write down what is given and what is required with units attached. Convert everything to compatible units before calculating. Use proportion when one quantity scales with another, and the dilution equation when only solvent is added.

For aliquots, dilute a weighed amount in a known volume and take the fraction that contains the required mass. This avoids weighing very small quantities on a balance that cannot measure them accurately.

Worked example

Prepare 100 mL of a 2 percent w/v solution from a 10 percent stock. C1 V1 = C2 V2 gives 10 x V1 = 2 x 100, so V1 = 20 mL of stock; add solvent to 100 mL. Check: 20 mL of a 10 percent solution contains 2 g, and 2 g in 100 mL is 2 percent.

  1. What does 5 percent w/v mean?
  2. How much stock solution is needed to make 500 mL of a 1 in 1000 solution from a 1 in 100 stock?
  3. Why use an aliquot method for 2 mg of a powder?

Answers: 1. 5 g per 100 mL. 2. 50 mL, made up to 500 mL. 3. Because 2 mg is below the reliable weighing range of most balances.

Physico-chemical processes in formulation

Formulation is applied physical chemistry. Solubility, dissolution, partitioning and surface tension decide whether a drug can be made into a stable, effective product.

Key ideas

  • Solubility depends on the drug, solvent, temperature, pH and crystal form.
  • The pH of a solution changes the ionisation of weak acids and bases, and ionised forms dissolve more readily in water.
  • Dissolution is the rate at which a solid drug enters solution; it often limits absorption.
  • Particle size influences dissolution rate: smaller particles dissolve faster.
  • Partition coefficient describes how a drug distributes between oil and water.
  • Surface tension matters for wetting, spreading and emulsion formation.
  • Surfactants lower surface tension and can form micelles.
  • Complexation and chelation change the apparent solubility of a drug.

How it works

Start by classifying the drug: is it acidic, basic or neutral, and is it water soluble? If it is a weak acid, raising the pH increases ionisation and solubility. If it is poorly soluble, reduce particle size, add a co-solvent, use a surfactant or form a salt.

Stability must be considered at the same time. A change that improves solubility may speed up degradation, so the formulation is always a compromise.

Check yourself

  1. Why does a weak base dissolve better at low pH?
  2. How does particle size affect dissolution?
  3. What is a surfactant?

Answers:1. Low pH protonates the base, making it ionised and more water soluble.2. Smaller particles have greater surface area and dissolve faster.3. A substance that lowers surface tension and aids wetting or emulsification.

Monophasic liquid dosage forms

A monophasic liquid is a single clear phase. Solutions, syrups, elixirs and aromatic waters are the common examples.

Key ideas

  • A solution is a homogeneous mixture of solute and solvent.
  • The solvent may be water, alcohol, a mixture, or a non-aqueous liquid.
  • Syrups contain a high concentration of sugar and resist microbial growth.
  • Elixirs are sweetened hydroalcoholic solutions used for drugs not soluble in water alone.
  • Aromatic waters are saturated solutions of volatile oils used as flavouring.
  • Solutions must be clear, stable and free from microbial contamination.
  • Preservatives are added when the formulation supports microbial growth.
  • Storage in a tight, light-resistant container protects the product.

How it works

Dissolve the drug in the appropriate solvent, add other ingredients in order, and make up to the final volume. Filter if particles remain. Check clarity, colour and odour before filling.

The pharmacist must also consider incompatibility: two substances in the same solution may react to form a precipitate, change colour or lose potency. If a reaction is expected, the ingredients are dispensed separately.

Check yourself

  1. Why does a syrup resist microbes?
  2. When would an elixir be chosen over an aqueous solution?
  3. Name two quality checks for a liquid preparation.

Answers:1. High sugar concentration discourages microbial growth.2. When the drug is not sufficiently soluble in water.3. Clarity and absence of particles or microbial contamination.

Suspensions and why particles settle

A suspension is a coarse dispersion of insoluble solid particles in a liquid. The drug is suspended rather than dissolved, so shaking before use is essential.

Key ideas

  • Suspensions are used for drugs that are insoluble or unstable in solution.
  • Sedimentation follows Stokes law: settling is faster for large, dense particles and slower for viscous vehicles.
  • A suspending agent increases viscosity and slows settling.
  • A wetting agent helps the liquid displace air from hydrophobic powder.
  • Flocculation forms loose clusters that settle quickly but redisperse easily.
  • Deflocculated particles settle slowly but form a hard cake that is difficult to redisperse.
  • A good suspension settles slowly, redisperses easily and pours well.
  • Preservatives and colouring may be added.

How it works

Wet the powder first, then add the vehicle containing the suspending agent. If the powder is hydrophobic, use a wetting agent such as a surfactant. Choose a flocculating agent when caking is a problem.

Stokes law explains every design decision: reduce particle size to slow settling, increase vehicle viscosity, or reduce the density difference between particle and liquid. The aim is not to prevent settling entirely but to ensure easy redispersion.

Check yourself

  1. Why must a suspension be shaken before use?
  2. According to Stokes law, what happens when particle size is reduced?
  3. What is the practical advantage of flocculation?

Answers:1. Because the particles settle on standing, so the dose would otherwise be uneven.2. Settling slows.3. The sediment is loose and redisperses easily.

Emulsions

An emulsion is a mixture of two immiscible liquids stabilised by an emulsifying agent. One liquid forms droplets inside the other.

Key ideas

  • Oil-in-water emulsions have oil droplets dispersed in water; water-in-oil emulsions have water droplets in oil.
  • The emulsifier forms a film around droplets and lowers interfacial tension.
  • Natural emulsifiers include gums and saponins; soaps and surfactants are synthetic options.
  • The HLB system classifies surfactants as hydrophilic or lipophilic and guides selection.
  • Creaming is the upward or downward movement of droplets and is reversible on shaking.
  • Cracking is irreversible separation into two layers.
  • Phase inversion changes an oil-in-water emulsion into a water-in-oil emulsion.
  • Microbial growth must be controlled, especially in oil-in-water products.

How it works

Choose the emulsion type first: oil-in-water spreads easily and is washable; water-in-oil is more occlusive and water-resistant. Then choose an emulsifier with the right balance. Mix the phases at the same temperature, add the emulsifier, and homogenise to reduce droplet size.

Cracking can result from the wrong emulsifier, extreme temperatures, added electrolytes or microbial growth. A creamed emulsion can be rescued by shaking; a cracked one cannot.

Check yourself

  1. What determines whether an emulsion is oil-in-water or water-in-oil?
  2. What is the difference between creaming and cracking?
  3. Why are oil-in-water emulsions more prone to microbial growth?

Answers:1. The emulsifier and the relative proportions of the phases.2. Creaming is reversible; cracking is not.3. The continuous aqueous phase supports microbial growth.

Incompatibilities

An incompatibility occurs when ingredients interact in a way that changes the appearance, strength or safety of a preparation.

Key ideas

  • Physical incompatibility gives a visible change such as precipitation or colour change.
  • Chemical incompatibility involves a reaction that reduces potency or forms a harmful product.
  • Therapeutic incompatibility arises from combining drugs with opposing or harmful effects.
  • A precipitate may form when a poorly soluble salt is produced.
  • Oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis and photolysis are common chemical reactions.
  • pH change is a frequent cause of precipitation.
  • Complexation may either help or harm solubility.
  • Some incompatibilities can be avoided by changing the order of mixing, the solvent or the container.

How it works

Read the prescription for pairs known to react. Predict the outcome from solubility and pH. If a precipitate is expected, try dilution, a change of vehicle, or dispensing the ingredients separately with clear instructions.

Document what was done and why. If the incompatibility cannot be resolved safely, contact the prescriber.

Check yourself

  1. Give one example of a physical incompatibility.
  2. How does pH cause precipitation?
  3. What should a pharmacist do when an incompatibility cannot be resolved?

Answers:1. A drug precipitating when two solutions are mixed.2. A pH change can convert a soluble ionised drug into its insoluble unionised form.3. Contact the prescriber and do not dispense an unsafe product.

Suppositories and surgical aids

Suppositories are solid dosage forms inserted into a body cavity, usually the rectum, where they melt or dissolve. Surgical aids include dressings, sutures and absorbent materials used in wound care.

Key ideas

  • Rectal suppositories avoid the stomach and partly avoid first-pass metabolism.
  • The base must melt at body temperature; cocoa butter and synthetic bases are common.
  • Displacement value is the amount of drug that displaces a unit weight of base.
  • Moulds are calibrated because the weight of a blank suppository varies.
  • Insertion technique affects retention and absorption.
  • Surgical aids must be sterile where they contact broken skin.
  • Dressings protect a wound, absorb exudate and reduce infection risk.
  • Sutures may be absorbable or non-absorbable.

How it works

Calculate the amount of base using displacement values, melt the base gently, mix in the drug, pour into cooled moulds and allow to set. Overheating can change the base and ruin the product.

For surgical aids, choose the material according to the wound: an absorbent dressing for a discharging wound, a non-adherent dressing for a fresh graft, and a sterile technique throughout.

Check yourself

  1. Why is displacement value needed in suppository calculation?
  2. At what temperature should a suppository base melt?
  3. Why must a dressing be sterile?

Answers:1. Because the drug takes the place of some base, so the base weight must be reduced.2. At or just below body temperature.3. Because it contacts broken skin and can introduce infection.

Atomic structure and bonding in pharmacy

Physical pharmaceutical chemistry begins at the atomic level because drug behaviour follows from structure.

Key ideas

  • An atom has a nucleus of protons and neutrons surrounded by electrons in shells.
  • The atomic number is the number of protons; the mass number is protons plus neutrons.
  • Electrons fill shells according to energy levels, and the outer shell determines bonding.
  • Ionic bonds form by electron transfer between atoms with very different electronegativity.
  • Covalent bonds form by sharing electrons.
  • Hydrogen bonds are weak attractions important in water, protein structure and drug-receptor binding.
  • Polarity determines solubility: polar drugs dissolve in water, non-polar drugs in lipids.
  • Oxidation state tracks electron transfer in reactions.

How it works

Draw the electronic configuration of the relevant atoms, then predict the bond type. A large difference in electronegativity gives an ionic bond; a small difference gives a covalent bond. If the molecule is asymmetric, it has a dipole and interacts with water.

This predicts solubility, stability and receptor binding. A drug that is too polar will not cross membranes; one that is too non-polar will not dissolve in blood.

Check yourself

  1. What determines the outer-shell electron count?
  2. Why does water dissolve ionic compounds?
  3. Name two types of bond in a drug molecule.

Answers:1. The atomic number and the filling rule for shells.2. Water is polar and stabilises the separated ions.3. Covalent and hydrogen bonds.

Metals in the body and inorganic medicines

Metal ions are essential in biology and useful in medicine, but the same ions can be toxic in excess.

Key ideas

  • Sodium and potassium maintain osmotic balance and nerve conduction.
  • Calcium is required for bone, muscle contraction and clotting.
  • Iron carries oxygen in haemoglobin.
  • Zinc, copper and magnesium act as enzyme cofactors.
  • Chelating agents bind metal ions to form rings and change their solubility and excretion.
  • EDTA is used to bind lead and calcium in poisoning and in laboratory anticoagulation.
  • Platinum complexes such as cisplatin are used in cancer treatment.
  • Heavy metals such as lead, mercury and arsenic are toxic.

How it works

A metal ion acts through its charge, size and ability to coordinate. Coordination chemistry explains why a chelator can remove a toxic metal: it forms a stable, soluble complex that the body can excrete.

In the laboratory, metal ions matter for enzyme assays and for reagents. In the clinic, they matter for deficiency, poisoning and drug design.

Check yourself

  1. How does a chelating agent work?
  2. Which metal is central to haemoglobin?
  3. Why are heavy metals toxic?

Answers:1. It binds the metal into a stable ring complex that can be removed.2. Iron.3. They bind to proteins and disrupt essential functions.

The human body: organisation, tissues, muscle and nerves

Anatomy and physiology give the pharmacist the map on which drugs act.

Key ideas

  • Levels of organisation run from chemical to cellular, tissue, organ, system and organism.
  • Homeostasis keeps the internal environment within narrow limits.
  • Four basic tissue types are epithelial, connective, muscle and nervous.
  • Epithelium covers surfaces and forms glands; connective tissue supports and binds.
  • Skeletal muscle is voluntary and striated; cardiac and smooth muscle are involuntary.
  • Neurons transmit electrical signals; glial cells support and protect them.
  • A synapse transmits signals between neurons using chemical messengers.
  • The central nervous system is brain and spinal cord; the peripheral nervous system is everything else.
  • The autonomic nervous system has sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions.

How it works

Structure predicts function and drug effect. A drug that blocks a receptor at a synapse changes signalling in the whole system. Understanding which tissue expresses the receptor explains both the therapeutic effect and the side effects.

The autonomic split is especially useful: sympathetic activity prepares for action, parasympathetic activity restores rest. Many drugs imitate or block one side of this system.

  1. List the four basic tissue types.
  2. Which division of the autonomic nervous system prepares the body for action?
  3. Why does receptor distribution matter?

Answers:1. Epithelial, connective, muscle, nervous.2. Sympathetic.3. It determines where a drug acts and what side effects appear.

Communication skills: words, sentences and paragraphs

Clear writing is a professional duty. A prescription, a label or a report that is ambiguous can harm a patient.

Key ideas

  • Word classes include nouns, pronouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, prepositions, conjunctions and interjections.
  • A sentence needs a subject and a finite verb to express a complete thought.
  • Concord means the subject and verb agree in number.
  • Ambiguity arises when a phrase has more than one possible meaning.
  • A paragraph develops one idea, usually through a topic sentence, support and a link.
  • Punctuation marks guide the reader through the structure.
  • Comprehension means reading for meaning, not for individual words.
  • A summary keeps the main points in fewer words and never adds new information.

How it works

Write the idea first, then choose the sentence that carries it most directly. Check subject-verb agreement, especially with long subjects. Place modifiers next to what they describe to avoid ambiguity.

For a summary, read the whole passage, list the main points in order, then write them in your own words. Do not copy whole sentences unless they are being quoted.

Check yourself

  1. Why is ambiguity dangerous in pharmacy?
  2. What is the topic sentence of a paragraph?
  3. What must a summary never do?

Answers:1. It can lead to the wrong drug, dose or instruction.2. The sentence that states the main idea of the paragraph.3. Add information that is not in the original.

Cognosy: plants, identification and metabolites

Cognosy is the study of medicines from natural sources, especially plants. It links botany, chemistry and pharmacology.

Key ideas

  • Plant taxonomy names and classifies plants using a binomial system of genus and species.
  • A herbarium is a collection of dried, pressed plant specimens used for identification.
  • Macroscopic examination looks at shape, size, colour, texture and odour.
  • Microscopic examination looks at tissue arrangement, hairs, stomata and crystals.
  • Ergastic contents are stored materials such as starch grains, aleurone, oils and calcium oxalate crystals.
  • Primary metabolites are needed for basic life: sugars, amino acids and nucleic acids.
  • Secondary metabolites are not essential to the plant but useful as drugs: alkaloids, glycosides, tannins, terpenes and flavonoids.
  • Some medicines come from animals, for example insulin and vaccines.

How it works

Identify a crude drug in stages: check the macroscopic features, then prepare a section or powder and look for diagnostic microscopic structures, then use chemical tests to confirm the class of metabolite. Reference a herbarium specimen when available.

Enzymes and their regulation

  • Coenzymes and cofactors are non-protein helpers; many come from vitamins.
  • Competitive inhibitors compete with the substrate and raise the apparent Km.
  • Non-competitive inhibitors reduce Vmax.
  • Enzyme activity is regulated by feedback inhibition, phosphorylation and gene expression.

How it works

Substrate concentration, pH, temperature and inhibitors determine reaction rate. At low substrate concentration the rate rises steeply; at high concentration the enzyme becomes saturated and the rate approaches Vmax.

Worked example

Sigma notation

Sigma notation is a compact way to write a long sum. Instead of listing every term, you give a rule for the terms and the range of the counting variable. The capital letter sigma means add up the terms that follow. A first-year student meets it in series, expectation, sums of squares and numerical integration.

Key ideas

  • The expression to the right of the sigma is the summand: the formula that generates each term.
  • The number below the sigma is the lower limit, and the number above is the upper limit.
  • The letter next to the sigma is the index of summation. It is a dummy variable: renaming it changes nothing.
  • A sum from k = 1 to n of k reads add k for k = 1, 2, 3, up to n.
  • Constants may be pulled outside the sigma; sums of two terms may be split into two sigmas.
  • The index need not start at 1. Always read the lower limit before you expand.

How it works

Identify the summand and the index. Substitute the lower limit, then every integer up to the upper limit, and add the results. If a shortcut exists, apply it and check it against a small case.

Two shortcuts earn a lot of marks. The sum of the first n positive integers is n(n+1)/2. The sum of their squares is n(n+1)(2n+1)/6, and the sum of their cubes is [n(n+1)/2]^2. Exam questions build harder sums from these.

A common trap is confusing the summand with the limit. In a sum from k = 1 to 5 of k^2, the exponent 2 belongs to the term while the 5 belongs to the range. Writing k^5 or stopping at 4 are both wrong.

Worked example

Find the sum from k = 1 to 12 of k, and check the formula on a smaller case. The formula gives 12 x 13 / 2 = 78. For n = 4 it gives 10, and 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 = 10, so the formula is being used correctly.

Now find the sum from k = 3 to 10 of 2k. This is the sum from 1 to 10 of 2k minus the sum from 1 to 2 of 2k: 110 - 6 = 104. Direct addition gives 6 + 8 + 10 + 12 + 14 + 16 + 18 + 20 = 104.

  3. A student says the sums with index k and index j are different because the letters differ. What is wrong?

Answers: 1. 5 + 8 + 11 + 14 = 38. 2. 20 x 21 x 41 / 6 = 2870. 3. The index is a dummy variable; both describe the same sum.

Sets, domains and equations

An equation is a statement that two expressions are equal. Solving it means finding every value of the variable that makes the statement true. Before you manipulate any equation you must know which values the variable may take: that allowed range is its domain.

Key ideas

  • A set is a collection of distinct objects called elements.
  • The natural numbers, integers, rational numbers and real numbers are nested sets. Every integer is rational; not every rational is an integer.
  • The domain of a variable in an equation is the set of values for which every expression is defined.
  • A denominator may not be zero. An even root of a negative real number is not defined in the real numbers. The argument of a logarithm must be positive.
  • The solution set is the set of all values that satisfy the equation.
  • An identity is true for every value in the domain; a conditional equation is true only for some values.
  • Extraneous solutions are values produced by algebra that fail when substituted back.

How it works

Write the domain first. Then simplify each side: expand brackets, collect like terms, and clear fractions by multiplying through by a common denominator. Whatever you do to one side you must do to the other.

Squaring both sides is a frequent source of extraneous solutions because it is not reversible: two different numbers can have the same square. Check every solution obtained after squaring in the original equation. The same warning applies when multiplying by an expression containing the variable, since that expression might be zero.

Exam questions often ask for a solution set in interval notation, so practise moving between set notation and intervals.

Worked example

Solve 1/(x - 2) = 3/(x + 1). The domain excludes x = 2 and x = -1. Multiply through by (x - 2)(x + 1): x + 1 = 3(x - 2), so 7 = 2x and x = 7/2. Check: the left side is (3/2)^(-1) = 2/3 and the right side is 3/(9/2) = 2/3. They agree.

Check yourself

  1. State the domain of 1/(x^2 - 9).
  2. Solve sqrt(x + 5) = x - 1 and explain why one candidate is rejected.
  3. How does an identity differ from a conditional equation?

Answers: 1. All real numbers except 3 and -3. 2. x = 4 or -1 from squaring, but -1 fails in the original; only 4 works. 3. An identity holds for every value in the domain; a conditional equation holds only for particular values.

Linear equations in one and two variables

A linear equation has no variable raised above the first power. Its graph is a straight line, and that geometric fact is the fastest way to understand the algebra.

Key ideas

  • A linear equation in one variable has the form ax + b = c, with a not zero.
  • A linear equation in two variables has the form ax + by = c.
  • Two equations in two unknowns form a system. A solution is a pair satisfying both at once.
  • Systems have one solution, no solution, or infinitely many solutions.
  • Parallel lines give no solution; identical lines give infinitely many.
  • Elimination removes a variable by adding scaled equations; substitution puts one expression into the other equation.

How it works

For a single equation, isolate the variable by undoing operations in reverse order. For a system, use substitution when one equation is easy to solve and elimination when coefficients line up.

Before solving, compare the ratios of coefficients. If a1/a2 equals b1/b2 but differs from c1/c2, the lines are parallel and there is no solution. If all three ratios are equal, the equations describe the same line.

Word problems need translation. Define the variables in words, write the equations, then interpret the answer in the units of the question.

Worked example

Solve 2x + 3y = 13 and 5x - y = 7. From the second equation y = 5x - 7. Substituting gives 2x + 15x - 21 = 13, so x = 2 and y = 3. Check: 4 + 9 = 13.

Quadratic equations and inequalities

A quadratic equation has the form ax^2 + bx + c = 0 with a not zero. Its graph is a parabola. There are three reliable methods: factorising, completing the square, and the quadratic formula.

Key ideas

  • Factorising works when the quadratic splits into two simple linear factors.
  • Completing the square rewrites the equation as (x + p)^2 = q.
  • The quadratic formula gives x = [-b +/- sqrt(b^2 - 4ac)] / (2a).
  • The discriminant b^2 - 4ac decides the roots: two real roots if positive, one repeated if zero, no real roots if negative.
  • The sum of the roots is -b/a and the product is c/a.
  • An inequality is solved by finding critical values and testing the intervals between them.
  • Multiplying or dividing an inequality by a negative number reverses the sign.

How it works

Try factorising first. Compute the discriminant before using the formula so you know what kind of answer to expect. For a quadratic inequality, move everything to one side, factorise, mark the roots on a number line and test one point in each interval.

The parabola opens upward when a is positive, so the expression is negative between the roots and positive outside. When a is negative the pattern reverses. Use this as a sanity check.

Worked example

Solve x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0 and then x^2 - 5x + 6 > 0. Factorising gives (x - 2)(x - 3) = 0, so x = 2 or 3. Testing x = 0 gives a positive value, x = 2.5 gives a negative value, x = 4 gives a positive value. Therefore the inequality holds for x < 2 or x > 3.

Check yourself

  1. Solve 3x^2 + 2x - 8 = 0.
  2. For what k does x^2 + kx + 9 = 0 have equal roots?
  3. Solve x^2 - x - 12 <= 0.

Answers: 1. Discriminant 100, so x = 4/3 or -2. 2. k = 6 or -6. 3. Between the roots: -3 <= x <= 4.

Indices and surds

Indices are shorthand for repeated multiplication. Surds are roots that cannot be simplified to a rational number. Both appear in logarithms, exponential equations and pharmaceutical calculations.

Key ideas

  • a^m times a^n equals a^(m+n); a^m divided by a^n equals a^(m-n).
  • (a^m)^n equals a^(mn); (ab)^n equals a^n b^n.
  • a^0 = 1 for non-zero a; a^(-n) = 1/a^n.
  • a^(m/n) means the nth root of a raised to the mth power.
  • sqrt(12) simplifies to 2 sqrt(3) by extracting the square factor.
  • Rationalising removes a surd from a denominator by multiplying top and bottom by the conjugate.
  • Like surds can be added; unlike surds cannot.

How it works

Write the base in prime-factor form, then apply the laws one at a time. The exponent laws only work when the bases are the same. For surds, extract the largest square factor. To rationalise 1/(a + sqrt(b)), multiply by (a - sqrt(b))/(a - sqrt(b)) so the denominator becomes a^2 - b.

Worked example

Simplify (2^5 x 2^3)/2^4: combine exponents to get 2^4 = 16. Rationalise 3/(2 - sqrt(3)): multiply by (2 + sqrt(3)) to get 3(2 + sqrt(3))/(4 - 3) = 6 + 3 sqrt(3).

Check yourself

  1. Simplify (x^3 y^2)^2/(x^2 y).
  2. Write sqrt(50) + sqrt(8) as one simplified surd.
  3. Rationalise 5/(sqrt(7) - sqrt(2)).

Answers: 1. x^4 y^3. 2. 7 sqrt(2). 3. sqrt(7) + sqrt(2).

Logarithms and exponential equations

A logarithm answers the question: to what power must I raise the base to get this number? If a^x = b, then log base a of b = x. Logarithms turn multiplication into addition and powers into multiplication.

Key ideas

  • log_a(b) = x means a^x = b, with a positive and not 1, and b positive.
  • log_a(1) = 0 and log_a(a) = 1.
  • log_a(mn) = log_a(m) + log_a(n); log_a(m/n) = log_a(m) - log_a(n).
  • log_a(m^n) = n log_a(m).
  • Change of base: log_a(b) = log_c(b)/log_c(a).
  • The common logarithm uses base 10; the natural logarithm uses base e.
  • Exponential equations are solved by taking logarithms of both sides.

How it works

If both sides can be written as powers of the same base, equate exponents and skip logarithms. Otherwise take logs of both sides and bring the exponent down with the power law. The argument of a logarithm must be positive, so discard any answer that breaks that rule.

Worked example

Solve 2^(3x - 1) = 32. Write 32 as 2^5, so 3x - 1 = 5 and x = 2. Check: 2^5 = 32.

Solve 5^x = 80: x = log(80)/log(5) = 2.723 to three significant figures.

Sequences, series and arithmetic progressions

A sequence is an ordered list of numbers; a series is the sum of its terms. Dosing intervals and any quantity changing by a fixed amount each step use this structure.

Key ideas

  • A sequence is a function whose domain is the positive integers.
  • In an arithmetic progression each term differs from the previous by a constant d.
  • The nth term is u_n = a + (n - 1)d.
  • The sum of the first n terms is S_n = n/2 [2a + (n - 1)d], also written n/2 (first + last).
  • The sum of the first n natural numbers is n(n+1)/2.
  • A linear sequence has a constant first difference; a quadratic sequence has a constant second difference.

How it works

Find a and d, write the nth-term formula, then substitute the given information. If two terms are given, solve two equations in a and d. The average of the first n terms must lie between the first and last terms; use that as a check.

Count carefully: from the 5th to the 11th term inclusive is 7 terms, so compute S_11 - S_4.

Worked example

An arithmetic progression has third term 7 and seventh term 19. Then a + 2d = 7 and a + 6d = 19, so d = 3 and a = 1. S_15 = 15/2 [2 + 42] = 330. Check with the last term 43: 15/2 (1 + 43) = 330.

Geometric progressions and infinite sums

In a geometric progression each term is multiplied by a constant ratio r to get the next. Growth, decay, compound interest and repeating decimals all use it.

Key ideas

  • The nth term is u_n = a r^(n-1).
  • The ratio is any term divided by the one before it.
  • The sum of the first n terms is S_n = a(1 - r^n)/(1 - r), for r not 1.
  • If |r| is less than 1, r^n tends to zero as n grows.
  • The sum to infinity is a/(1 - r) when |r| < 1.
  • If |r| is 1 or more, the sum to infinity does not exist.

How it works

Find r first. Before applying the infinite-sum formula, check |r| < 1. When two terms are given, divide the larger-term equation by the smaller to eliminate a. If r comes from an even power, consider both signs unless the question restricts them.

Worked example

A GP has sixth term eight times its third term and the sum of the seventh and eighth terms is 192. Dividing u_6 by u_3 gives r^3 = 8, so r = 2. Then a(64 + 128) = 192, so a = 1. The sum from term 5 to 11 is (2^11 - 1) - (2^4 - 1) = 2032. Listing the terms 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024 confirms 2032.

The binomial theorem

The binomial theorem expands powers of a two-term expression and gives the binomial distribution its probabilities.

Key ideas

  • (x + y)^n equals the sum over k from 0 to n of nCk x^(n-k) y^k.
  • The coefficients nCk are the entries of Pascals triangle.
  • The kth term is called the general term.
  • For (x - y)^n the signs alternate.
  • The coefficients sum to 2^n.
  • For non-integer n the expansion is infinite and needs |y| less than |x|.

How it works

Write the general term, substitute n and k, then simplify. To find one coefficient, solve for k using the power of x rather than expanding everything. For a term independent of x, set the exponent of x to zero.

Worked example

Straight lines and coordinate geometry

Coordinate geometry turns geometric questions into algebra. A point is fixed by an ordered pair; a line is fixed by its gradient and one point.

Key ideas

  • The distance between (x1, y1) and (x2, y2) is the square root of (x2-x1)^2 + (y2-y1)^2.
  • The midpoint averages the corresponding coordinates.
  • The gradient m is the change in y divided by the change in x.
  • y = mx + c has gradient m and cuts the y-axis at c.
  • Parallel lines have equal gradients; perpendicular gradients multiply to -1.
  • The line through (x1, y1) with gradient m is y - y1 = m(x - x1).

How it works

Convert whatever information you have into y = mx + c or the point-gradient form. Solve two line equations together to find their intersection. For a perpendicular through a point, form the perpendicular line and intersect it with the original.

Sketch the situation roughly. A computed intersection in the wrong quadrant is a signal to recheck signs.

Worked example

Find where x + 2y - 3 = 0 meets 4x + 5y - 9 = 0, then find the line through that point perpendicular to 3x + 2y - 5 = 0. Substituting x = 3 - 2y into the second gives y = 1 and x = 1, so P = (1, 1). The third line has gradient -3/2, so the perpendicular gradient is 2/3. Through (1, 1): 2x - 3y + 1 = 0. Check at (1,1): 2 - 3 + 1 = 0.

Trigonometry: ratios, radians and identities

Trigonometry begins with side ratios in a right triangle and extends to any angle. It is essential for vectors, waves and oscillations.

Key ideas

  • sine = opposite/hypotenuse, cosine = adjacent/hypotenuse, tangent = opposite/adjacent.
  • sin^2(theta) + cos^2(theta) = 1 and tan(theta) = sin(theta)/cos(theta).
  • One full turn is 2 pi radians, so pi radians is 180 degrees.
  • Arc length is r theta and sector area is (1/2) r^2 theta, with theta in radians.
  • Exact values come from the 30-60-90 and 45-45-90 triangles.
  • The sine and cosine rules extend the ratios to any triangle.

How it works

Label the angle and the sides first, then choose the ratio linking what you know to what you need. For angles beyond 90 degrees, use the quadrant signs and reduce to an acute reference angle. To prove an identity, work from the more complicated side and write everything in terms of sine and cosine.

Worked example

A 10 m ladder makes 60 degrees with the ground. Height = 10 sin(60) = 5 sqrt(3), about 8.66 m. The base distance is 5, and 5^2 + (5 sqrt(3))^2 = 100 = 10^2, so the result is consistent.

Matrices and determinants

A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers. It packages systems of equations and linear transformations into one object.

Key ideas

  • An m by n matrix has m rows and n columns.
  • Addition and subtraction require equal orders and work entry by entry.
  • In multiplication, entry (i, j) is the dot product of row i of the first and column j of the second.
  • Multiplication needs columns of the first to equal rows of the second, and is not commutative.
  • The identity matrix has ones on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere.
  • The determinant of [[a, b], [c, d]] is ad - bc.
  • An inverse exists only when the determinant is non-zero.

How it works

For a 2 by 2 inverse, swap the diagonal entries, negate the off-diagonal entries, and divide by the determinant. Write a system as AX = B and solve with X = A^(-1)B. For larger systems, row-reduce the augmented matrix to reduced row-echelon form.

Worked example

Invert A = [[3, 1], [4, 2]] and solve 3x + y = 5, 4x + 2y = 9. The determinant is 2, so the inverse is [[1, -0.5], [-2, 1.5]]. Multiplying by [5, 9] gives x = 0.5 and y = 3.5. Check: 1.5 + 3.5 = 5 and 2 + 7 = 9.

Check yourself

  1. Find the determinant of [[5, 2], [3, 1]].
  2. Why need AB not equal BA?
  3. Solve [[2, 1], [1, 1]] [x, y] = [7, 5].

Answers: 1. -1. 2. Transformations do not commute. 3. x = 2, y = 3.

Differentiation

A derivative measures how fast a function changes at a point: the gradient of the tangent to the curve.

Key ideas

  • The derivative is the limit of [f(x+h) - f(x)]/h as h tends to zero.
  • Power rule: derivative of x^n is n x^(n-1).
  • Product rule: (uv)' = u'v + uv'. Quotient rule: (u/v)' = (u'v - uv')/v^2.
  • Chain rule: derivative of f(g(x)) is f'(g(x)) g'(x).
  • Implicit differentiation attaches dy/dx to terms containing y.
  • A stationary point has first derivative zero; the second derivative classifies it.

How it works

Simplify into power terms and differentiate term by term. Use the chain rule whenever one function sits inside another. For stationary points, set the derivative to zero and check the second derivative: positive means a minimum, negative a maximum.

Worked example

Differentiate 3x^4 + 2x^(-1) - 5: f'(x) = 12x^3 - 2x^(-2), so f'(1) = 10. Numerically, [f(1.001) - f(1)]/0.001 is about 10.006, close enough. For y = x^2 sin(x), dy/dx = 2x sin(x) + x^2 cos(x).

Check yourself

  1. Differentiate (2x + 3)^5.
  2. Classify the stationary point of x^2 - 6x + 5.
  3. Find dy/dx when x^2 + y^2 = 25.

Answers: 1. 10(2x + 3)^4. 2. x = 3 is a minimum at (3, -4). 3. dy/dx = -x/y.

Integration

Integration reverses differentiation and measures accumulated quantity: area under a curve, distance from velocity, or drug exposure from concentration over time.

Key ideas

  • An indefinite integral is a family of antiderivatives plus a constant C.
  • Integral of x^n is x^(n+1)/(n+1) for n not -1.
  • Integral of 1/x is ln|x|.
  • Substitution reverses the chain rule; parts reverses the product rule.
  • A definite integral gives a number: F(b) - F(a).
  • Area below the x-axis counts negative unless total area is requested.

How it works

Simplify the integrand first. Use substitution when a function and its derivative both appear; use parts for a polynomial multiplied by a logarithm, exponential or trigonometric function. Always differentiate your answer to check it.

Worked example

Integrate 3x^2 + 2x from 0 to 2: antiderivative x^3 + x^2 gives 8 + 4 = 12. Integrate x e^x by parts: x e^x - e^x + C = e^x(x - 1) + C; differentiating returns x e^x.

What statistics is and how variables are measured

Statistics is the discipline of turning observations into decisions. For a laboratory scientist it covers the design of a study, the summary of results, and the judgement of whether a difference is real or could have arisen by chance.

Key ideas

  • Descriptive statistics organise and summarise data; inferential statistics use a sample to make statements about a population.
  • A population is the whole group of interest; a sample is the part actually measured.
  • A variable is any quantity that can take different values.
  • A qualitative variable records a category; a quantitative variable records a number.
  • Nominal categories have no order; ordinal categories have an order but unequal spacing.
  • Interval data have equal spacing but no true zero; ratio data have equal spacing and a true zero.
  • A discrete variable takes isolated values; a continuous variable can take any value in a range.

How it works

Classify every variable before choosing a summary. Counts and percentages suit nominal data; medians suit ordinal data; means and standard deviations suit numerical data where the distribution is roughly symmetric. Using a mean on nominal categories is meaningless even though a computer will produce a number.

A common exam question asks which scale a given measurement uses. Temperature in degrees C is interval because zero does not mean no temperature. Haemoglobin concentration in g/dL is ratio because zero means none and twice the value is twice the amount.

Worked example

A study records sex, blood group, haemoglobin concentration and disease stage. Sex and blood group are nominal; disease stage is ordinal; haemoglobin is ratio. Only haemoglobin may be summarised with a mean and standard deviation.

Check yourself

  1. Name the scale used for pain scores of none, mild, moderate, severe.
  2. Why is a mean invalid for blood group?
  3. Give one example each of a discrete and a continuous variable in a laboratory.

Answers: 1. Ordinal. 2. Blood groups are unordered categories, so there is no numerical quantity to average. 3. Discrete: number of white cells counted in a chamber. Continuous: packed cell volume.

Data collection and questionnaire design

Good analysis cannot rescue bad data. The way a question is worded and the way subjects are chosen determine what the numbers mean.

Key ideas

  • Primary data are collected directly for the study; secondary data already exist.
  • A questionnaire should use short, neutral, unambiguous questions.
  • Leading questions push the respondent toward an answer and bias the result.
  • Closed questions are easy to code; open questions give richer detail but are harder to analyse.
  • A pilot study tests the instrument before the main data collection.
  • Non-response bias arises when people who refuse differ systematically from those who answer.
  • A question about a sensitive topic should be worded carefully and placed late.

How it works

Decide first what each question is meant to measure, then write the question to capture exactly that. Avoid double-barrelled questions such as asking about diet and exercise in one item, because a respondent may agree with only one part.

Check that every possible answer has a category, including "dont know" where appropriate. Make categories mutually exclusive so one response cannot fit two boxes.

Worked example

The question "Do you agree that good doctors always exercise and eat well?" is leading, double-barrelled and absolute. A better pair of questions asks separately: "How many days per week do you exercise?" and "How many servings of vegetables do you eat per day?"

Check yourself

  1. What is the difference between primary and secondary data?
  2. Why should a questionnaire be piloted?
  3. Rewrite the question "You support the new policy, dont you?" neutrally.

Answers: 1. Primary data are gathered by the investigator; secondary data were collected by someone else. 2. To find confusing or ambiguous items before the real study. 3. "Do you support the new policy? Yes / No / Undecided."

Sampling methods

Sampling chooses which members of a population will be observed. The method affects both the accuracy of estimates and the strength of conclusions.

Key ideas

  • A sampling frame is the list from which the sample is drawn.
  • Simple random sampling gives every member an equal chance of selection.
  • Systematic sampling selects every kth member after a random start.
  • Stratified sampling divides the population into groups and samples within each group.
  • Cluster sampling selects whole groups, then samples within them.
  • Convenience sampling is easy but biased because the sample is not representative.
  • Sampling error is the difference between the sample result and the true population value due to chance.
  • Non-sampling error includes measurement mistakes, bias and non-response.

How it works

Choose the method that matches the question. Stratified sampling improves precision when the population contains distinct groups such as male and female students or different year groups. Cluster sampling is cheaper when members are naturally grouped, for example by class or locality.

With systematic sampling, check that the sampling interval does not line up with a hidden pattern in the frame. If every 10th name happens to be a class representative, the sample will be biased.

Worked example

To estimate mean haemoglobin in 600 Level 100 students, use a sampling frame of all registered students. A stratified sample of 60 students, with 30 drawn from each sex, is better than picking 60 volunteers from a corridor, because it guarantees representation and avoids volunteer bias.

Check yourself

  1. When is stratified sampling preferable to simple random sampling?
  2. What is the difference between sampling error and bias?
  3. Why is convenience sampling weak evidence?

Answers: 1. When the population has identifiable subgroups that differ on the measured variable. 2. Sampling error is random and falls with larger samples; bias is systematic and does not. 3. The sample may differ from the population in important ways.

Organising data: frequency tables and charts

Raw data are hard to interpret. A frequency table arranges observations into classes, and a chart turns those counts into a picture.

Key ideas

  • A frequency table lists classes together with the number of observations in each.
  • For grouped data, the class midpoint is used as a representative value.
  • Class boundaries should not overlap and should cover all observations.
  • A histogram uses bars whose widths reflect class intervals; the bars touch.
  • A bar chart has gaps and is used for categories.
  • A pie chart shows proportions of a whole.
  • A cumulative frequency curve plots running totals against upper class boundaries.

How it works

Decide the number of classes, then choose a class width that covers the range. Count carefully, using tally marks so no observation is missed. Compute relative frequency by dividing each count by the total; cumulative frequency is the running sum.

A histogram differs from a bar chart in an important way: unequal class widths must be reflected in unequal bar widths, and the height then represents frequency density rather than frequency. This is a common examination trap.

Worked example

Twenty patients have haemoglobin values grouped as 8-10, 10-12, 12-14, 14-16 with counts 3, 7, 8, 2. Relative frequencies are 0.15, 0.35, 0.40, 0.10, and cumulative frequencies are 3, 10, 18, 20. The modal class is 12-14 because it has the highest count.

  1. Why do histogram bars touch while bar chart bars do not?
  2. What value represents a class when computing a grouped mean?
  3. How do you find the median from a cumulative frequency curve?

Answers: 1. Histogram classes are continuous; bar chart categories are separate. 2. The class midpoint. 3. Find half the total frequency on the vertical axis and read the corresponding value.

Measures of centre

A measure of centre locates the typical value of a dataset. The mean, median and mode answer slightly different questions.

Key ideas

  • The mean is the sum of all values divided by the count.
  • The median is the middle value after sorting; with an even count it is the average of the two middle values.
  • The mode is the most frequent value.
  • For grouped data, the approximate mean uses class midpoints weighted by frequencies.
  • The mean uses every value and is sensitive to outliers.
  • The median resists extreme values.
  • The mode is the only suitable average for nominal data.

How it works

Sort the data before finding the median or mode. For the grouped mean, multiply each midpoint by its frequency, add the products, and divide by total frequency.

If a distribution is skewed, the mean is pulled toward the long tail. In a right-skewed set, mean exceeds median; in a left-skewed set, mean is below median. This relationship is often asked directly.

Worked example

For values 2, 3, 3, 5, 8: mean = 21/5 = 4.2, median = 3, mode = 3. If the 8 is replaced by 80, the median and mode stay at 3 while the mean becomes 93/5 = 18.6, showing how the mean responds to an outlier.

Grouped example: classes with midpoints 9, 11, 13, 15 and frequencies 3, 7, 8, 2 give a mean of (27 + 77 + 104 + 30)/20 = 238/20 = 11.9.

  1. Find the mean, median and mode of 4, 4, 7, 9, 11.
  2. Which average is best for a variable with extreme outliers?
  3. In a right-skewed distribution, which is larger, mean or median?

Answers: 1. Mean 7, median 7, mode 4. 2. The median. 3. The mean.

Measures of spread

Spread describes how tightly values cluster around the centre. Two datasets can have the same mean but very different variability.

Key ideas

  • The range is the largest value minus the smallest and is easily distorted by outliers.
  • Variance is the average squared deviation from the mean.
  • For a sample, divide the sum of squared deviations by n - 1; for a population, divide by n.
  • Standard deviation is the square root of variance and has the same units as the data.
  • Coefficient of variation is standard deviation divided by mean, expressed as a percentage.
  • Interquartile range is the difference between the upper and lower quartiles and resists outliers.

How it works

Compute the mean, subtract it from each value, square the differences, add them, and divide by n - 1. Take the square root for the standard deviation. Use the sample formula unless the question explicitly says these are all the values in the population.

For grouped data, deviations are taken from class midpoints. The coefficient of variation is useful when comparing variability between measurements with different units or very different means.

Worked example

Values 10, 12, 12, 14, 16 have mean 12.8. Squared deviations: 7.84, 0.64, 0.64, 1.44, 10.24, summing to 20.8. Sample variance = 20.8/4 = 5.2, and sample standard deviation = sqrt(5.2) = 2.28. Check: all deviations are within about two standard deviations, which is sensible for five values.

  1. Why is standard deviation preferred to variance for reporting?
  2. Compute the sample variance of 2, 4, 4, 4, 6.
  3. When is the coefficient of variation more useful than standard deviation?

Answers: 1. It is in the original units. 2. Mean 4, sum of squares 8, divide by 4 gives 2. 3. When comparing datasets with different units or very different means.

Probability basics and counting

Probability attaches a number between 0 and 1 to the chance of an event. Counting rules supply those numbers when all outcomes are equally likely.

Key ideas

  • An experiment has a set of possible outcomes called the sample space.
  • An event is a subset of the sample space.
  • Classical probability assumes equally likely outcomes: favourable outcomes divided by total outcomes.
  • Relative frequency probability comes from observed proportions over many trials.
  • The complement of an event has probability 1 minus the probability of the event.
  • For mutually exclusive events, the probability of either is the sum of their probabilities.
  • For independent events, the probability of both is the product.
  • nPr counts ordered arrangements; nCr counts unordered selections.

How it works

Write the sample space explicitly for small problems. For larger ones, identify whether events are mutually exclusive and whether they are independent; these are different ideas and students often confuse them.

Use counting rules when the question involves selecting or arranging people or objects. If order matters use permutations; if it does not use combinations.

Worked example

A bag has 5 red and 3 blue balls. Two are drawn without replacement. The probability of two red balls is (5/8)(4/7) = 20/56 = 5/14. The draws are not independent because the first draw changes the composition; the multiplication still works because it is a conditional probability.

Check yourself

  1. What is the probability of at least one head in two fair coin tosses?
  2. In how many ways can 3 people be chosen from 8?
  3. Are being female and being a laboratory scientist mutually exclusive?

Answers: 1. 3/4. 2. 8C3 = 56. 3. No, a person can be both.

Conditional probability and independence

Conditional probability asks how the chance of an event changes when you know that another event has happened. It is the foundation of diagnostic testing.

Key ideas

  • The probability of A given B is the probability of A and B divided by the probability of B.
  • Independence means the probability of A given B equals the probability of A.
  • For independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) P(B).
  • Mutually exclusive events with positive probability cannot be independent.
  • A tree diagram keeps track of conditional branches.
  • A contingency table displays joint counts and is the usual starting point for these questions.

How it works

Identify the conditioning event: it is the one after the word given, and it becomes the denominator. Build a tree or a two-way table, then multiply along branches for joint probabilities and add across branches for totals.

Test independence numerically by comparing P(A given B) with P(A). If they differ, the events are associated.

Worked example

In a group of 100 students, 60 are female and 20 of those study statistics. If a student is chosen at random, P(statistics given female) = 20/60 = 1/3. If P(statistics) overall is 30/100 = 0.30, then 1/3 is not equal to 0.30, so sex and studying statistics are not independent in this group.

  2. If P(A) = 0.4, P(B) = 0.5 and P(A and B) = 0.2, are A and B independent?
  3. Why can mutually exclusive events not be independent?

Answers: 1. The chance of A when B is known to have occurred. 2. Yes, because 0.4 x 0.5 = 0.2. 3. If A occurs, B cannot occur, so knowing A changes the chance of B to zero.

Bayes theorem and screening tests

Bayes theorem updates a probability when new evidence arrives. In medicine it converts a tests sensitivity and specificity into the chance that a positive result means true disease.

Key ideas

  • Bayes theorem: P(D given positive) = P(positive given D) P(D) divided by P(positive).
  • Sensitivity is the probability of a positive test in a person who has the disease.
  • Specificity is the probability of a negative test in a person without the disease.
  • Positive predictive value is the chance that a positive result is a true positive.
  • Negative predictive value is the chance that a negative result is a true negative.
  • Prevalence is the pre-test probability of disease.
  • A rare disease produces many false positives even with a highly specific test.

How it works

Build a two-way table using a convenient hypothetical population, often 1000 or 10000 people. Fill the diseased and non-diseased rows from prevalence, then split each row using sensitivity and specificity. The positive predictive value is true positives divided by all positives.

This table method is safer than plugging into a formula because it makes the denominators visible.

Worked example

A test has sensitivity 0.90 and specificity 0.95 in a population where prevalence is 2 percent. Out of 10000 people, 200 have the disease: 180 test positive. Of the 9800 without disease, 490 test positive falsely. Positive predictive value = 180/(180 + 490) = 180/670 = 0.269. So only about 27 percent of positives are true cases. Repeating the test or testing a higher-risk group raises this figure.

Check yourself

  1. What does sensitivity measure?
  2. Why does low prevalence reduce positive predictive value?
  3. A test is 99 percent sensitive and 95 percent specific in a population with 10 percent prevalence. Estimate the positive predictive value using 1000 people.

Answers: 1. The proportion of true positives among people with the disease. 2. False positives from the large healthy group dominate the true positives. 3. 100 diseased give 99 positives; 900 healthy give 45 false positives; PPV = 99/144 = 0.688.

Discrete random variables and expectation

A random variable assigns a number to each outcome. A discrete random variable takes isolated values and is described by a probability distribution.

Key ideas

  • A probability mass function lists the probability of each possible value.
  • Probabilities must be non-negative and sum to 1.
  • Expected value E(X) is the weighted average of values: sum of x times P(X = x).
  • E(X^2) is the sum of x^2 times P(X = x).
  • Variance is E(X^2) minus [E(X)]^2.
  • For independent variables, E(X + Y) = E(X) + E(Y).
  • The cumulative distribution gives P(X less than or equal to x).

How it works

Write the distribution as a table and check that probabilities sum to 1 before computing anything. Multiply each value by its probability and add for the expectation. For variance, compute E(X^2) first, then subtract the square of the mean; this is usually faster than summing squared deviations.

Interpretation matters: expectation is a long-run average, not a value that must occur in a single trial.

Worked example

A variable takes values 0, 1, 2 with probabilities 0.2, 0.5, 0.3. E(X) = 0 + 0.5 + 0.6 = 1.1. E(X^2) = 0 + 0.5 + 1.2 = 1.7. Variance = 1.7 - 1.21 = 0.49.

Check yourself

  1. What must the probabilities in a distribution sum to?
  2. Find E(X) for values 1 and 3 with probabilities 0.25 and 0.75.
  3. Is E(X) always a possible value of X?

Answers: 1. 1. 2. 2.5. 3. No; it is a long-run average.

Binomial and Poisson distributions

These two discrete distributions cover counts of successes and counts of events.

Key ideas

  • A binomial experiment has a fixed number n of identical trials, each with the same success probability p, and independent outcomes.
  • The probability of r successes is nCr p^r (1-p)^(n-r).
  • Binomial mean is np and variance is np(1-p).
  • A Poisson distribution counts events in a fixed interval when events occur independently at a constant average rate.
  • Poisson probability is e^(-lambda) lambda^x / x!.
  • Poisson mean and variance are both lambda.
  • The Poisson approximates the binomial when n is large and p is small.

How it works

Check the conditions before choosing a distribution. If there is a fixed number of trials with two outcomes, use binomial. If there is a fixed time, area or volume with no fixed upper count, use Poisson.

For at least or at most questions, either sum the relevant terms or use the complement. "At least one" is almost always fastest as 1 minus the probability of zero.

Worked example

In a group of 20 patients where the probability of a reaction is 0.05, the probability of exactly one reaction is 20C1 (0.05)(0.95)^19 = 0.377. The probability of at least one is 1 - 0.95^20 = 0.642. Check: the mean number of reactions is 20 x 0.05 = 1, so a probability near 0.64 for at least one is plausible.

  1. State two conditions for a binomial experiment.
  2. For Poisson with mean 3, find the probability of zero events.
  3. What is the variance of a binomial with n = 50 and p = 0.2?

Continuous random variables and the normal distribution

A continuous variable can take any value in an interval. The normal distribution is the most important continuous model because many biological measurements are approximately normal.

Key ideas

  • A continuous distribution is described by a density curve; area under the curve gives probability.
  • The total area under a density curve is 1.
  • The normal distribution is symmetric and bell shaped, with mean mu and standard deviation sigma.
  • About 68 percent of values lie within one standard deviation of the mean, 95 percent within two, and 99.7 percent within three.
  • A z-score standardises a value: z = (x - mu)/sigma.
  • The standard normal has mean 0 and standard deviation 1.
  • Normal tables give the area to the left of a z-score.

How it works

Draw the curve and shade the required area before computing anything. Convert the value to a z-score, look up the table, then adjust: for the area to the right subtract from 1; for the area between two values subtract the smaller area from the larger.

For reverse problems, find the z-score from the table first, then unstandardise with x = mu + z sigma.

Worked example

Haemoglobin values are normal with mean 12 g/dL and standard deviation 2. What proportion are below 9? z = (9 - 12)/2 = -1.5. The table gives 0.0668, so about 6.7 percent are below 9. Check: 9 is below the mean, so the answer must be less than 0.5, which it is.

Check yourself

  1. What is the total area under a density curve?
  2. Find P(Z between -1 and 1) approximately.
  3. A normal variable has mean 50 and standard deviation 10. Find the value with 5 percent above it.

Answers: 1. 1. 2. About 0.68. 3. z = 1.645 gives x = 66.45.

Standard error and confidence intervals

A sample mean is only an estimate. The standard error measures its uncertainty, and a confidence interval puts plausible bounds around the population mean.

Key ideas

  • The standard error of the mean is sigma divided by the square root of n.
  • Larger samples give smaller standard errors.
  • A confidence interval has the form estimate +/- critical value times standard error.
  • For a 95 percent interval with known variance the critical value is about 1.96.
  • When sigma is unknown and the sample is small, use the t distribution with n - 1 degrees of freedom.
  • A 95 percent confidence interval means that the method captures the true value in 95 percent of repeated samples.
  • A wider interval reflects more uncertainty.

How it works

Decide whether sigma is known. If it is, use the normal critical value; if not, use t. Compute the standard error from the sample standard deviation and sample size. Multiply by the critical value and add and subtract from the mean.

Do not confuse standard deviation with standard error. Standard deviation describes spread among individuals; standard error describes uncertainty in the mean.

Worked example

A sample of 64 students has mean systolic pressure 128 mmHg and standard deviation 16. The standard error is 16/8 = 2. The 95 percent interval is 128 +/- 1.96 x 2, which is 124.08 to 131.92. Check: the interval is centred on 128 and its width shrinks as the sample grows.

Check yourself

  1. What happens to standard error when the sample size is quadrupled?
  2. Why use t rather than z for a small sample with unknown sigma?
  3. What does 95 percent confidence mean?

Answers: 1. It halves. 2. t accounts for extra uncertainty in estimating sigma. 3. The procedure captures the true value in 95 percent of repeated samples.

Hypothesis testing

A hypothesis test decides whether observed data provide enough evidence to reject a claim about a population.

Key ideas

  • The null hypothesis states no effect or no difference; the alternative states the researched effect.
  • The test statistic measures how far the estimate is from the null value in standard error units.
  • A significance level, often 5 percent, sets the threshold for rejecting the null.
  • The p-value is the probability of data as extreme as observed if the null is true.
  • A small p-value is evidence against the null; it is not the probability that the null is true.
  • A one-sided test looks in one direction; a two-sided test looks in both.
  • A type I error rejects a true null; a type II error fails to reject a false null.

How it works

State the hypotheses, choose the test, compute the statistic, find the p-value, and compare with the significance level. Then write a conclusion in the language of the problem, not just reject or fail to reject.

For a mean with known variance, z = (sample mean - claimed mean)/(sigma/sqrt(n)). With unknown variance and small n, use t with n - 1 degrees of freedom.

Worked example

A claimed mean is 100 with sigma 15. A sample of 36 gives mean 106. The standard error is 15/6 = 2.5, so z = (106 - 100)/2.5 = 2.4. For a two-sided test at 5 percent the critical values are +/-1.96, so 2.4 exceeds the threshold and the null is rejected. The p-value is about 0.016.

Check yourself

  1. What is a type I error?
  2. If p = 0.03 at the 5 percent level, what is the decision?
  3. Does a non-significant result prove the null hypothesis?

Answers: 1. Rejecting a true null. 2. Reject the null. 3. No; it only means the evidence was insufficient.

Chi-square tests

Chi-square tests compare counts with expected counts. They are used for goodness of fit and for association between two categorical variables.

Key ideas

  • Goodness of fit asks whether observed counts match a claimed distribution.
  • A test of association asks whether two categorical variables are independent.
  • Expected count in a contingency table is row total times column total divided by grand total.
  • The statistic is the sum over cells of (observed - expected)^2 / expected.
  • Degrees of freedom are number of categories minus one for goodness of fit, and (rows - 1)(columns - 1) for association.
  • A large statistic means observed counts differ from expectation more than chance alone would suggest.
  • Expected counts below about 5 make the approximation unreliable.

How it works

Build the expected table first and check that its total equals the observed total. Compute each contribution to the statistic, add them, then compare with a critical value or find the p-value from the chi-square distribution with the correct degrees of freedom.

Worked example

A 2 by 2 table of treatment and outcome has counts [[45, 15], [15, 25]]. Row totals are 60 and 40; column totals are 60 and 40; grand total 100. Expected counts are 36, 24, 24, 16. Contributions are 81/36 + 81/24 + 81/24 + 81/16 = 14.0625. With one degree of freedom this is beyond the 0.05 critical value of 3.84, so the variables are associated.

  3. Why are small expected counts a problem?

Answers: 1. Row total times column total divided by grand total. 2. (3-1)(4-1) = 6. 3. The chi-square approximation becomes poor.

Correlation and regression basics

Correlation measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship. Regression goes further and gives an equation for prediction.

Key ideas

  • The correlation coefficient lies between -1 and 1.
  • A positive value means both variables rise together; a negative value means one falls as the other rises.
  • A value near zero means no linear relationship, not no relationship of any kind.
  • Correlation does not establish causation.
  • The regression line has the form y = a + bx, where b is the slope.
  • The slope is the change in y for a one-unit increase in x.
  • Prediction outside the observed range is extrapolation and is unsafe.

How it works

Plot the data first. A curved pattern or an outlier can make a correlation coefficient misleading. Fit the line, interpret the slope in context, and only predict within the range of the data collected.

An outlier can create or destroy an apparent correlation, so always inspect the scatter plot before reporting a number.

Worked example

A regression of weight on height gives y = -80 + 0.9x with x in centimetres and y in kilograms. A person 170 cm tall is predicted to weigh -80 + 153 = 73 kg. The slope means each additional centimetre is associated with 0.9 kg more weight in this sample. This is association, not proof that height causes weight.
